The Build Trap: Why I Kept Perfecting a Product Nobody Was Buying

Muhammad Tufail Abbas Founder @ SMBify
Jul 31, 2026 • 8 Min Read
Key Founder Lesson: No amount of code polish or UI refinement fixes a market validation problem. A product sitting in isolation with an empty dashboard is not proof that the software is bad. It is proof that the process around customer acquisition was never built.

The Illusion of Progress: The Story of SiteGenie

A while back I built SiteGenie, an AI website builder, as a side project.

I was genuinely proud of it. The user interface was clean, the AI generation pipeline worked smoothly, and every single feature I added made the platform feel more polished. Day after day, I kept telling myself that the product just needed one more improvement, one more template, or one more dashboard refinement, and then people would naturally start signing up.

They never did.

It was not because the tool was bad. The software genuinely worked. The real problem was that I never left my desk long enough to find out who actually wanted it, what specific problem they would pay for, or where they were even looking for a solution. I was so deep in the building process that marketing and sales felt like uncomfortable distractions from what I considered the real work.

That was the build trap, and I did not see it while I was inside it.

Warning for Technical Founders: Writing code provides an instant, controllable feedback loop. A test passes or fails. A bug gets fixed and the error disappears. Sales and marketing are messy and unpredictable. Retreating back into building code to avoid uncomfortable sales rejection is the fastest way to build software nobody buys.

Why Technical Builders Fall Into This Trap

If you come from a technical background, you understand why this trap is so easy to walk into. Writing code gives you instant, honest feedback. It compiles or it does not. A test passes or it fails. A bug gets fixed and the error disappears. That feedback loop feels satisfying because it is completely controllable.

Marketing and sales do not work that way. You reach out to a potential customer and they might ignore you. You post about your product and nobody responds. You ask for feedback and hear that the problem you spent weeks solving was not painful enough for anyone to pay money for. That kind of uncertainty is deeply uncomfortable, especially when compared to the clean logic of a codebase.

So the natural instinct is to retreat back into building. Add one more feature. Fix one more edge case. Convince yourself that the next update is the one that finally makes people care.

With SiteGenie, I repeated this cycle for months. I now know that no amount of polish fixes a validation problem.

Vibe Coding Made This Trap Even Easier to Fall Into

AI coding tools like Cursor, Claude, and Gemini have made building faster than ever before. What used to take weeks of writing boilerplate code now takes an afternoon of prompting. That is genuinely useful, but it also removed one of the few natural brakes that used to slow builders down.

Before AI coding assistants existed, if you wanted to add a new feature, you had to sit down and manually write the code, which required significant time and effort. That friction occasionally forced a pause to ask whether the feature was even worth building in the first place.

Now with vibe coding tools, that friction is mostly gone. You can describe an idea and have working code in minutes, so it becomes very easy to keep shipping features without ever stepping back to ask if anyone actually wants them.

With SiteGenie, this was a major part of the problem. Once AI coding tools entered the picture, every improvement felt cheap to build, so I kept building. The dashboard, the AI generation flow, the templates, all of it kept getting refined because refining was fast and easy. Meanwhile the hard question, whether anyone outside my own head wanted this tool, kept getting pushed to later.

Vibe coding did not cause the build trap. But it removed the natural friction that used to slow builders down enough to notice they were inside one.

The Question I Had to Ask Myself

At some point I had to be completely honest about what I was actually doing. Was I building a business, or was I avoiding the harder, messier work of finding real customers?

There is nothing wrong with building for the love of building. Plenty of good software exists purely as a craft or a hobby. But if the goal is a real business, the product is only one part of the machine. The other part is a repeatable way of getting it in front of people who have the problem and are willing to pay to solve it.

That is the part I had skipped entirely with SiteGenie.

What Changed When I Started SMBify

When I started SMBify, I approached the entire business model differently on purpose. Before writing copy or building fulfillment deliverables, I spent time understanding who the client actually was: home service contractors like plumbers, HVAC companies, roofers, and electricians who needed to show up in local search and Google Maps but had no time or expertise to manage that themselves.

I treated customer acquisition the same way I used to treat a technical problem. Instead of guessing, I tested. Instead of building in isolation, I looked at what was already working for similar businesses and adjusted based on real signals, not assumptions.

The shift was not simply "get better at sales." It was building an actual system around getting in front of the right people consistently, the same way I would build a technical system for anything else. A system has clear inputs, a repeatable process, and a measurable output. Once I started treating client acquisition that way, it stopped feeling like an unpredictable, emotional gamble and started feeling like something I could manage and improve.

The Takeaway for Business Owners and Contractors

A product or website that nobody buys from is not proof that the offer is bad. Often it is proof that the process around getting in front of customers was never built.

SiteGenie taught me that lesson the hard way, and it is the exact reason SMBify exists today with a clear, repeatable service model instead of another perfect tool sitting quietly with an empty dashboard.

If you are a contractor or local business owner and your website is in the same position, built well but invisible in local Google search, that is exactly the gap SMBify was built to close.
