The Build Trap: Why I Kept Perfecting a Product Nobody Was Buying

From building SiteGenie in isolation to founding SMBify. Read founder Tufail Abbas on escaping the build trap and building a repeatable Local SEO system for contractors.

Muhammad Tufail Abbas July 31, 2026 6 min read

🟢 Key Founder Lesson

No amount of code polish fixes a validation problem. A product sitting quietly with an empty dashboard is not proof that the software is bad. It is proof that customer acquisition was never built.

A while back I built SiteGenie, an AI website builder, as a side project.

I was proud of it. The interface was clean, the AI generation worked well, and every feature I added made it feel more polished. I kept telling myself the product just needed one more improvement and people would start signing up.

They never did.

Not because the tool was bad. It genuinely worked. The problem was that I never left my desk long enough to find out who actually wanted it, what they would pay for, or where they were even looking for a solution like this. I was so deep in building that marketing felt like a distraction from the "real work."

That was the build trap, and I did not see it while I was in it.

🔴 Why Builders Retreat Into Code

Writing code gives instant, controllable feedback. Sales and marketing do not. When potential customers ignore you, the instinct is to retreat back into building code.

Why Builders Fall Into This Trap

If you come from a technical background, you understand why this trap is so easy to walk into. Writing code gives you instant, honest feedback. It compiles or it does not. A test passes or it fails. A bug gets fixed and the error disappears. That feedback loop feels good because it is controllable.

Marketing and sales do not work that way. You reach out to a potential customer and they might ignore you. You post about your product and nobody responds. You ask for feedback and hear that the problem you solved was not painful enough for anyone to pay for. That kind of uncertainty is uncomfortable, especially compared to the clean logic of a codebase.

So the instinct is to retreat back into building. Add one more feature. Fix one more edge case. Convince yourself that the next update is the one that finally makes people care.

With SiteGenie, I did this for months. I now know that no amount of polish fixes a validation problem.

Vibe Coding Made This Trap Even Easier to Fall Into

Tools like Claude, Gemini, and Cursor have made building faster than ever. What used to take weeks of writing boilerplate now takes an afternoon of prompting. That is genuinely useful, but it also removed one of the few natural brakes that used to slow builders down.

Before, if you wanted to add a feature, you had to sit down and actually write it, which took time and effort. That friction occasionally forced a pause to ask whether the feature was even worth building. Now with AI coding tools, that friction is mostly gone. You can describe an idea and have working code in minutes, so it becomes very easy to keep shipping features without ever stepping back to ask if anyone wants them.

With SiteGenie, this was part of the problem. Once AI coding assistants entered the picture, every improvement felt cheap to build, so I kept building. The dashboard, the AI generation flow, the templates, all of it kept getting refined because refining was fast and easy. Meanwhile the actual hard question, whether anyone outside my own head wanted this tool, kept getting pushed to "later."

Vibe coding did not cause the build trap. But it removed the friction that used to slow builders down enough to notice they were in one.

The Question I Had to Ask Myself

At some point I had to be honest about what I was actually doing. Was I building a product, or was I avoiding the harder, messier work of finding customers?

There is nothing wrong with building for the love of building. Plenty of good software exists purely as a craft or a hobby. But if the goal is a business, the product is only one part of the machine. The other part is a repeatable way of getting it in front of people who have the problem and are willing to pay to solve it.

That is the part I had skipped entirely with SiteGenie.

What Changed When I Started SMBify

When I started SMBify, I approached it differently on purpose. Before writing a single word of copy or building any deliverable, I spent time understanding who the client actually was: home service contractors like plumbers, HVAC companies, roofers, and electricians who needed to show up in local search and Google Maps but had no time or expertise to manage that themselves.

I treated customer acquisition the same way I used to treat a technical problem. Instead of guessing, I tested. Instead of building in isolation, I looked at what was already working for similar businesses and adjusted based on real signals, not assumptions.

The shift was not "get better at sales." It was building an actual system around getting in front of the right people consistently, the same way I would build a system for anything else. A system has clear inputs, a repeatable process, and a measurable output. Once I started treating client acquisition that way, it stopped feeling like an unpredictable, emotional gamble and started feeling like something I could actually manage and improve.

The Lesson

A product that nobody buys is not proof that the product is bad. Often it is proof that the process around it was never built. SiteGenie taught me that lesson the hard way, and it is the reason SMBify exists with a clear, repeatable service model instead of another perfect tool sitting quietly with an empty dashboard.

If you are a contractor or business owner and your website is in the same position, built well but invisible in search, that is exactly the gap SMBify was built to close.
